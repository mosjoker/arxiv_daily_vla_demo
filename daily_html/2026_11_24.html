WeaveVLA: 3D Relational Grounding for Vision-Language-Action Models

Vision-language-action (VLA) models leverage pretrained vision-language representations for instruction-conditioned robot control, yet their understanding of task-relevant 3D relations and how these relations evolve during manipulation remains largely implicit. To address this challenge, we introduce \textbf{WeaveVLA}, a framework that enhances VLA action generation by explicitly modeling task-relevant relative 3D relations and maintaining relational context across observations. WeaveVLA employs a task-conditioned sparse graph neural network to encode relative 3D relations among objects, the gripper, and nearby references. Pair-specific recurrent edge states integrate geometric evidence across observations to maintain evolving spatial context. A learned-query readout summarizes the updated graph representations into compact relational tokens, which condition the action expert alongside the original vision-language context. An auxiliary geometry reconstruction objective further encourages spatial structure preservation. We instantiate WeaveVLA on StarVLA-GR00T and $π_{0.5}$, achieving over 98\% average success on LIBERO with both backbones. On LIBERO-Plus, WeaveVLA improves success from 74.9\% to 82.0\% and from 85.7\% to 87.8\%, respectively, with additional improvements on CALVIN D$\rightarrow$D. On three real-world tasks, the StarVLA-GR00T-based variant improves average success from 55.6\% to 70.0\% under standard conditions and from 33.3\% to 53.3\% under OOD perturbations, surpassing the $π_{0.5}$ baseline in both settings.

RUIBench: What Makes a Good Robot-Use Interface?

Foundation models can reason about complex physical tasks, yet translating this intelligence into reliable robot execution remains challenging. We argue that a critical, often overlooked factor is the robot-use interface (RUI), which determines how agents express and execute physical actions. To systematically study its impact, we introduce RUIBench, comprising 30 manipulation tasks across five suites spanning world knowledge, in-context learning, discovery, dynamic interaction, and contact-rich manipulation. We further develop RUIKit, a unified framework that crosses six action abstractions with two interaction forms, yielding 13 interfaces under shared perception and control. In simulation, changing only the RUI shifts a frontier agent's success rate from 45% to 81%, and that of a fine-tuned 4B model from 13% to 47%. Our analysis reveals that effective interfaces reduce unnecessary decisions while preserving essential feedback, with their benefits shaped by agent behavior and physical uncertainty. We further uncover a verification gap, where agents frequently declare success despite observable failures, revealing the need for task-level verification beyond individual actions. Together, these findings establish RUI design as a critical factor in embodied agent reliability.

Leakage-Safe Auditing of Attribution-Driven Data Curation in Medical Imaging

When can an attribution-derived anomaly justify changing a medical-imaging training set? We formulate attribution-driven curation as three separately testable claims: anomaly detection, harmfulness estimation, and intervention utility. We test the first and third using a patient-disjoint, identifier-preserving audit of a 3D convolutional classifier for computed tomography angiography aneurysm candidates. We identify 232 exactly zero-valued arrays among 1,201 positive patches, including 170 training positives, and invalidate a historical 321-patch attention-failure subgroup whose identifier-free records were joined to files by position. On the held-out test set, GradCAM++ spatial concentration is central for every candidate class, reversing the historical pattern. GradCAM++ and Integrated Gradients show negative rank-order agreement for positives, limiting method-independent anatomical interpretation. Across five paired training seeds, removing the 170 corrupted training positives yields AUC 0.9617 +/- 0.0044 versus 0.9652 +/- 0.0039 for full-data training (difference -0.0035; 95% CI -0.0082 to 0.0007). Excluding 34 raw-zero test positives widens rather than closes the gap, countering a blank-memorization explanation. Budget-matched random-removal controls suggest the result is not simply an effect of reducing training size. Input corruption does not necessarily imply that deletion improves generalization. Our framework makes leakage control, sample identity, and intervention testing explicit in attribution-driven medical-imaging data curation.

Wrist-Camera Video Diffusion as Generalizable Robot Policies

A generalist manipulation policy should perform new tasks, operate in unfamiliar scenes, and transfer skills across robot embodiments. We present Wrist Video Policy (WVP), a closed-loop manipulation framework that learns how manipulation unfolds through video prediction and recovers executable motion through geometric estimation. Our key idea is to represent robot motion through wrist-camera trajectories, which determine the corresponding end-effector poses through a fixed hand-eye transformation. We pair a video generator that predicts future wrist views with a pose estimator adapted from a pretrained 3D model to recover the corresponding camera trajectories. Model stitching maps the generator's predicted latents directly into the estimator's intermediate feature space, bypassing RGB decoding. This decomposition allows task knowledge to expand through broad human and robot video data while reusing geometric motion estimation across tasks and embodiments. The resulting pose estimator transfers across Franka Panda, PiPER-X, and Galaxea R1 Pro without further fine-tuning. Across tabletop and laundry-room settings, WVP generalizes to tasks ranging from precise grasping and inserting to handling deformable objects, achieving a success rate of over 50% in zero-shot out-of-distribution evaluations. Our project page is at https://wrist-video-policy.github.io.

UMAMI: A Multimodal Benchmark Dataset for Evidence-Grounded Dietary Decisions

Dietary decisions require identifying a food's nutrient content and considering individual dietary needs, but recognizing food from an image alone does not provide this information. Visually similar foods can have different nutrient profiles, so selecting the wrong food record can lead to incorrect decisions even when nutrient rules are applied correctly. Furthermore, satisfying nutrient constraints does not necessarily establish a food's relevance to a person's intake. Evaluating these challenges together requires connecting food images, nutrient records, and individual intake histories. To support this evaluation, we introduce UMAMI (Unified Multimodal Assessment of Meals and Intake), a multimodal dataset and benchmark linking independently collected food images with nutrient profiles and respondent health and intake records. Using this dataset, we construct three diagnostic tasks covering visual nutrient question answering, personalized food recommendation, and condition-aware food substitution. Paired instances and input controls examine how visual identity and respondent context affect decisions. Our experiments identify errors in both food-record selection and nutrient-rule application, while showing that higher rule compliance does not necessarily improve alignment with subsequent recorded intake. These findings highlight the need to evaluate how multimodal systems select and use structured evidence for dietary decisions. The dataset, code, and dashboard are available at https://umami-benchmark.github.io.

Authors: Safal Thapaliya, Jiatan Huang, Debapriya Chatterjee, Yanfang Ye, Chuxu Zhang

RoFE: Rotated Frozen Embeddings for Low-Data Multimodal Tabular Learning

Multimodal tabular learning combines tabular data with images and text, often in settings where labeled data are scarce. Tabular foundation models such as prior-data fitted networks (PFNs) offer a promising solution via in-context learning without task-specific training. However, modern image and text encoders produce embeddings with hundreds or thousands of dimensions, far exceeding the number of features encountered during PFN pretraining. Existing approaches bridge this gap using principal component analysis (PCA) to compress frozen embeddings. Surprisingly, we find that this PCA interface can underperform simple linear classifiers when labeled examples are scarce, even though PFNs are designed for small datasets. We identify the underlying failure mechanism: the PFN's per-column standardization removes the variances of the PCA scores. When the PCA keeps every direction of the labeled examples, the examples collapse into a regular simplex in which all pairwise distances are equal. To address this problem, we propose Rotated Frozen Embeddings (RoFE), a training-free method that rotates PCA scores before PFN inference. We theoretically show that rotation makes the column variances nearly equal, so that standardization approximately preserves the embedding geometry. Across image and text classification tasks, encoders, and tabular foundation models, RoFE improves low-data performance over the PCA interface and outperforms baselines that require task-specific training. The improvements also extend to regression.

Distill the Correction, Not the Rewrite: Hindsight Twins for Self-Improving Text-to-Image Models

Text-to-image models can learn from their own mistakes by distilling a self-teacher conditioned on a vision-language critic's revised prompt. However, such self-teachers barely improve alignment, and both they and their hindsight-contrast variants absorb the critic's unintended additions to the scene, such as lighting or scenery. We attribute this to two factors: (1) the prompt already specifies the desired fix, so a useful revision must go beyond the failed span, often rewriting the entire scene and adding details that overshadow the fix; (2) although the critic localizes the error to a single span, self-teachers condition on the rewrite, entangling the intended fix with unintended additions. Motivated by these insights, we propose Hindsight Twins (HinT), which fills the critic's revision template once with the intended fix and once with the observed error and distills only their velocity difference, anchored at the user's prompt. Because text encoders spread the edit of a single slot to all other tokens, HinT further aligns the twins' token states outside the slot. Across Qwen-Image-2512, SD3.5-M, and FLUX.2-klein, HinT keeps the rate of critic-induced bias at the base-model level while matching or exceeding the alignment gains of prior self-distillation, and it keeps improving over twelve rounds of self-training where contrastive baselines decay.

Déjà VAE: When Do Autoencoder Traces Reveal Generated Images?

A latent diffusion model's output has already passed through its autoencoder, so a second round trip changes it very little. This "déjà vu" underlies reconstruction-based detectors of generated images; we analyse when it reveals a generated image and when it does not. Read by a frozen vision foundation model without training, the trace is strongest in intermediate layers, and, unlike pixel-level reconstruction error, it treats blur as a sign of a real image. It belongs to the generator's exact decoder: on Synthbuster, the exact decoders of Stable Diffusion 1.3, 1.4, 2 and XL are the strongest of eight autoencoders and catch 94-100% of their generators' images at a 5% false-alarm rate (SDXL's version identified post hoc), while near-identical versions catch 7-42%. A detector trained only on real photographs and their autoencoder copies matches one trained on 162k generated images on GenImage (+2.3 points, 95% CI [0.9, 3.7]) and complements it in fusion, but one resize with JPEG re-save, or a mild blur, costs it 19-27 points. Two lessons concern benchmarks. The in-the-wild benchmark Chameleon can be solved from image width alone. And on Synthbuster, how the real photographs are matched to the generated images' size decides whether our detector leads or trails (+4.4 or -9.7 points): the matching protocol alone can reverse a detector comparison. Code and per-image scores: https://github.com/iamsaugatpandey/deja-vae

Token Pruning Needs a Protected Sink Token: Coherent Spatial Aggregation in Vision Transformers

Vision Transformers (ViTs) are a predominant architecture for computer vision, with token reduction emerging as a popular paradigm to improve their efficiency. Prior work compares reduction algorithms, but not how models respond to them, nor how their response changes with model depth. We propose Monte Carlo Token Pruning (MCTP), which estimates each token's impact on the prediction when tokens are explicitly removed during the forward pass. Applying MCTP to recent pretrained models, we observe that most, such as DINOv3, maintain a similar ranking of token importance at every depth (high Coherent Aggregation), while SigLIP 2 does not. This coherence requires predictions to survive pruning, which depends on keeping high-norm "sink" tokens: prefix tokens are kept by design, while prefix-free SigLIP 2 forms them on patch tokens, which pruning may remove. We show that in removing DINOv3's prefix tokens, its predictions and coherence collapse, while protecting only 5 tokens for SigLIP 2 cuts the token impact outside the object's bounding box by up to 62%. Beyond this, MCTP offers a post-hoc method to evaluate how a pretrained ViT responds to pruning, and suggests that token reduction methods should keep "sinks".

WildAnySplat: Feed-Forward 3D Gaussian Splatting in the Wild

We present WildAnySplat, a feed-forward 3D Gaussian Splatting framework that enables robust novel-view synthesis from in-the-wild multi-view images. Unlike controlled captures, real-world images often contain multi-view inconsistencies caused by transient occluders, illumination changes, and camera ISP, which degrade the quality of existing feed-forward reconstruction methods. To address this challenge, we introduce a student-teacher framework that learns transient-robust and appearance-consistent 3D representations. A teacher network operates on clean images, while a student processes the corresponding in-the-wild inputs. The teacher provides clean supervision to guide the student toward a consistent canonical representation. Since paired clean and in-the-wild data are scarce, we use a hybrid data strategy, combining real paired captures, synthetic augmentations, and pseudo-clean labels generated from optimization-based reconstructions, bridging the domain gap between synthetic and real-world data. We adopt a transformer architecture with dedicated DPT heads for camera pose, depth, and Gaussian prediction. We additionally predict per-view transient masks to reweight photometric supervision and suppress unreliable Gaussians. To account for cross-view appearance variation, we incorporate a bilateral grid module that models per-image color transformations and enables recovery of a canonical radiance representation. Experiments on challenging in-the-wild benchmarks show that WildAnySplat improves robustness to transient occlusions and appearance variation while retaining the fast inference of feed-forward Gaussian Splatting.

OmniV-Bench: Can MLLMs Comprehend Immersive Spatio-temporal Scenes?

Omnidirectional videos (ODVs) capture dynamic environments with a full $360^\circ$ field of view, providing rich spatial context and temporal dynamics for applications such as virtual reality (VR), augmented reality (AR), and embodied intelligence. Recent multimodal large language models (MLLMs) have made great progress in spatial and temporal understanding, yet their ability to comprehend the immersive 360 spatio-temporal information conveyed by ODVs remains largely unexplored. To bridge this gap, we first introduce OmniV-Bench, a Benchmark designed to comprehensively evaluate the capabilities of MLLMs on spatio-temporal Omnidirectional Video understanding. OmniV-Bench contains 4,123 high-quality ODVs collected from diverse scenes, along with 8,923 human-annotated question-answer pairs spanning 11 fine-grained tasks across three categories, i.e., general, spatial, and spatio-temporal understanding. Extensive benchmark experiments reveal that the spatio-temporal complexity of ODVs poses significant challenges for current MLLMs. To address these limitations, we further propose OmniV-Harness, an agentic harness that enhances ODV understanding through viewpoint-guided evidence initialization and task-relevant evidence acquisition with adaptive tool use. Comprehensive experiments demonstrate that OmniV-Harness consistently improves immersive spatio-temporal reasoning ability of MLLMs. Both OmniV-Bench and OmniV-Harness will be released upon publication.

Parsing Visual Latents for Multimodal Spatial Reasoning

Spatial reasoning across multiple views requires inferring the 3D relations between objects and the camera from 2D observations, which depends on cross-view correspondence and scene geometry that vision-language models (VLMs) do not reliably extract. Pretrained vision encoders already capture this information in their latent features, with CLIP linking objects to image regions, DINOv3 matching semantics across views, and VGGT encoding multi-view geometry. However, VLMs do not benefit from these features when we present them directly in context as grayscale feature images. We therefore propose LatentParse, a training-free framework that parses latent features into explicit spatial evidence for VLMs. In LatentParse, an analyst VLM probes the features of all three encoders with predefined tools that locate objects, track them across views, and measure motion between views. It then distills the results into a concise text report, which is provided together with the images to the VLM to answer the question. We evaluate LatentParse on MMSI-Bench, MV-RoboBench and SPAR-Bench, where it improves all five VLMs over the photo-only baseline by 0.7-2.4 percentage points on average. On MMSI-Bench, it raises Qwen3.8-27B from 34.4% to 39.5%, with the largest improvements of 19.2 percentage points on the camera-related questions. These results suggest that latent features become useful for spatial reasoning once they are parsed into relation evidence, without training. Code is available at https://github.com/Harvard-AI-and-Robotics-Lab/LatentParse.

Demographic Encoding is Not Exposure: Fairness and Privacy in Chest X-ray Foundation Models

Medical foundation models encode patient demographics and can reveal whether a patient was in the training set, and these properties are often treated as one problem. We tested whether they are aligned by measuring demographic encoding, diagnostic fairness gaps and membership-inference vulnerability on the same models. We trained 200 linear or multilayer-perceptron (MLP) heads on frozen BiomedCLIP, DINOv2 and RAD-DINO embeddings, with three seeds, and audited them on CheXpert and NIH ChestX-ray14. Demographic encoding explained almost no variance in patient vulnerability, and all 72 CheXpert associations were statistically equivalent to zero. Erasing linearly decodable demographics left leakage unchanged but reduced age-related underdiagnosis gaps. Leakage depended instead on head capacity and training length. On RAD-DINO, a linear head reached a membership-inference area under the curve (AUC) of 0.694, compared with 0.907 for a 30-epoch MLP, at similar test performance. Fairness and privacy should therefore be audited separately, and linear heads or shorter training reduce leakage with little or no loss of utility. The code is available on GitHub for public use.

Reinforcing Diffusion Models by Instantaneous Trajectory Distribution Matching

Forward diffusion reinforcement learning (RL)---enabling deterministic ODE sampling without trajectory backpropagation---has grown increasingly popular for efficiently aligning diffusion models. However, we reveal that existing methods essentially rely on signed reward-weighted regression (RWR), regressing the \emph{trainable policy} velocity onto rollouts from a \emph{behavior distribution}. Unbiased baseline subtraction implicitly assumes that the policy velocity equals the behavior marginal velocity---an idealized assumption violated under classifier-free guidance (CFG) rollouts, offline/off-policy updates, and imperfect online self-consistency. This \emph{behavior--policy mismatch} breaks baseline invariance and injects \emph{baseline-induced gradient drift}, destabilizing training. To resolve this issue, we propose \textbf{I}nstantaneous \textbf{T}rajectory \textbf{D}istribution \textbf{M}atching (\textbf{iTDM}), taking the infinitesimal limit of Trajectory Distribution Matching toward a time-local reward-guided target via the posterior-score identity to yield a sample-level gradient anchored at the behavior velocity without learning an additional reward-tilted target score. Tracking this velocity with an online auxiliary denoiser guarantees exact baseline invariance at the auxiliary optimum, effectively suppressing baseline drift across both online and offline RL. Extensive experiments across compositional generation, text rendering, and preference alignment show that iTDM achieves state-of-the-art in-domain and out-of-domain performance. Notably, on the challenging GenEval2 benchmark, iTDM converges nearly $3\times$ faster than competing methods while scaling smoothly to $0.87$ without collapse. Code is available at https://github.com/Luo-Yihong/iTDM

Graph2Scene: Relation-Aware Text-to-Image Generation with Graph-Guided Diffusion Model

Text-to-image diffusion models still struggle with complex prompts containing many elements and interactions. Existing attention-control methods mainly focus on controlling where elements appear, but often ignore how objects interact with each other. As a result, relation tokens describing interactions between elements may assign high attention to irrelevant regions, leading to missing or incorrect interactions. In this paper, we propose Graph2Scene, a training-free framework that treats scene graphs as a core control signal for diffusion generation. It represents elements as nodes and their relations as edges, and translates these element-relation dependencies into selective attention guidance. It grounds each element to its corresponding region and guides relational interactions among the involved elements. To achieve appropriate relation control without over-constraining the generation process, Graph2Scene further adapts the control duration to scene complexity and progressively relaxes graph guidance during denoising. Experiments on T2I-CompBench and ConceptMix show clear improvements in compositional generation, especially for object relations. On ConceptMix, Graph2Scene improves relationship accuracy from 0.59 to 0.84 over the strongest FLUX.1-based regional-control baseline. Results on LongBench-T2I further show its advantage on long and complex prompts.

Foresight Forcing: Learning Spatial Foresight for Robotic Manipulation

Robotic manipulation policies can improve action prediction by anticipating how objects move and how their spatial relationships change. World Action Models (WAMs) learn to predict future visual observations, but their training objectives typically leave the underlying 3D geometric transitions implicit. To alleviate this problem, we introduce Foresight Forcing ($\mathcal{F}^{2}$), a WAM that learns a spatial transition representation from changes in a pretrained 3D feature space. During training, a frozen 3D foundation model extracts patch-wise feature differences from aligned current and future observations that provide dense supervision for geometry-aware feature transitions. We distill these targets into spatial transition queries, which are progressively refined from the current observation, language instruction, and proprioceptive state, and use them alongside current visual features to generate actions. At deployment, the policy predicts the queries without future observations or the 3D teacher. Experiments on LIBERO, LIBERO-Plus, and RoboTwin2.0 demonstrate strong manipulation performance and improved robustness, including 95.01% average success on RoboTwin2.0 Full. On six real-world manipulation tasks, $\mathcal{F}^{2}$ achieves a mean task score of 85.8%.

Self-Evolving Evaluation Environments for Computer-Use Agents

Computer-use agents (CUAs) are rapidly improving, yet their evaluation remains largely static. Existing benchmarks provide a strong foundation, but fixed difficulty ceilings, limited feature coverage, and coarse aggregate scores progressively weaken their discriminative and diagnostic value. We introduce CUA-SEE, a closed-loop evaluation environment that co-evolves tasks and executable verifiers from rollout evidence. It disentangles three objectives: benchmark evolution expands evaluation scope through cohort-calibrated difficulty and coverage growth; diagnostic evolution increases resolution through agent-conditional capability-boundary probes; and verifier evolution improves precision by translating changing requirements into state-grounded checkpoints with adversarial validation. Across multiple agents, applications, and rounds, CUA-SEE generates challenging, measurable tasks while preserving process-level evidence. Our results position adaptive evaluation as a scalable counterpart to self-evolving agents, continuously tracking what agents can do, where their boundaries lie, and how reliably progress is measured.

Diffutoon-2: Toon Shading with Video Diffusion Models

Toon shading renders objects with a flat, stylized appearance. Diffusion models open a new route to this task by transforming photorealistic videos into an anime style. However, existing approaches often rely on curated source and style training pairs or additional visual references. Diffutoon avoids these requirements through structure guidance and colorization, but builds on an image diffusion backbone with short-window motion modules and repeated overlapping-window denoising. We present Diffutoon-2, a video diffusion framework that learns toon shading through animation reconstruction. Two complementary modules independently reconstruct animation clips from outlines and blurred colors, with the video backbone frozen. At inference, the modules jointly guide generation using the same types of conditions extracted from photorealistic footage, requiring neither cross-domain training pairs nor external style references. Extensive experiments demonstrate that Diffutoon-2 outperforms both general-purpose and domain-specific baselines, across quantitative metrics and human evaluation.

SIAMBA: A Pseudo-Siamese Mamba-based Deep Learning Network for Multimodal Flood Change Detection

Flood change detection from bi-temporal multispectral and synthetic aperture radar (SAR) images is essential for rapid disaster response. In this context, Sentinel-2 multispectral imagery provides discriminative spectral observations, whereas Sentinel-1 SAR provides weather-independent structural information. Using these modalities jointly requires modelling temporal evidence while accounting for cross-modal heterogeneity. In particular, change detection is complicated by cloud contamination, illumination variability, acquisition geometry and speckle, and heterogeneous land-cover responses. To address these challenges, we propose SIAMBA, a pseudo-siamese Mamba-based network for bi-temporal flood change detection from paired Sentinel-1 and Sentinel-2 observations. SIAMBA uses dedicated modality-specific encoders with dual-pooling squeeze-and-excitation recalibration and selective state-space processing for long-range spatial context. A gated cross-modal change module derives absolute, signed, and relative log-ratio differences at multiple semantic levels, and attention-refined skip connections support progressive multi-scale decoding. Experiments are carried out on OMBRIA benchmark, which includes an independent test set and geographically diverse subsets in France, Albania, Guyana, and Timor. SIAMBA achieves 81.98% IoU, and 93.57% OA on the independent test set, reaching the highest values in the considered comparison. It also provides the highest unweighted average IoU and OA across four regional subsets spatially disjuncted. These results indicate that combining selective state-space contextual modelling with gated multiscale discrepancy learning effectively improves flood change mapping across the evaluated geographic settings.

FGSCalib: Targetless Camera-LiDAR Extrinsic Calibration from Foreground Segmentation

Camera-LiDAR pairs installed as fixed infrastructure need periodic recalibration, but a calibration target takes the installation out of service. We present FGSCalib, a targetless method that calibrates a stationary camera-LiDAR pair from the objects moving in its view. We only assume that arbitrary moving objects are present in the scene: each sensor separates the moving foreground from the static background, without relying on object classes. With the correct extrinsic, the LiDAR foreground points project onto the camera foreground. FGSCalib turns the camera mask into a boundary-proximity field and finds the extrinsic by two-stage simulated annealing over selected frame pairs. It needs no measured initial extrinsic (random starts give similar results) and works with both rotating and non-repetitive LiDARs. We evaluate it on 21 simulated scenarios with exact ground truth and on 72 real ones (nine camera-LiDAR pairs in eight scenes), with a reference averaged from 43 manual calibrations. In simulation the median error is 5.3 cm and 0.19°. On real data we use a tolerance of 18 cm and 1.8°, met by 93% of single manual calibrations. Keeping the lowest-cost of five runs, FGSCalib meets it in 51 of the 72 scenarios: in 20 of 24 with the Livox, where the strongest of five baselines reaches 11, and in 31 of 48 with the two rotating LiDARs, where only one baseline estimate does. Its median error is 9.5 cm and 0.9°, against 7.3 cm and 0.7° for a single manual calibration. The code and the dataset are available online.

CoSel: Cooperation as Selection for End-to-End Driving

Cooperative driving can extend a vehicle's awareness beyond its own sensors, but existing cooperative end-to-end methods inject shared information into the planner and rely on cooperative driving demonstrations to learn how to use it. The moments in which shared information should change a driving decision are rare in such data, providing limited supervision for learning this behavior. We argue that cooperation need not be learned by the planner. Instead, reacting to shared information requires predicting how surrounding agents will move and selecting a response from the candidate trajectories of an ego planner. We introduce CoSel, a cooperation-as-selection framework that leaves the planner and its ego-only input unchanged. A separately trained cooperative predictor uses observations from the ego vehicle, roadside units, and connected automated vehicles to predict surrounding agents and their future motion. CoSel evaluates the planner's candidate trajectories against these predictions, preserves the planner's choice when it remains safe, and selects the closest safe alternative when shared information reveals additional risk. Each safety intervention is traceable to the shared evidence that triggers it. In closed-loop evaluation, CoSel achieves a driving score of 70.7 on V2Xverse, outperforming the best compared cooperative method by 7.5 points, and the highest average driving score of 85.8 on MDrive. Under missing ego detections, the collision count of CoSel increases only from 17 to 19 in 72 scenarios, compared with 19 to 32 for an existing cooperative end-to-end model.

Do Waste Classifiers Recognise Items They Have Never Seen? Object-Disjoint Evaluation on Household and Medical Waste

Automatic waste classification supports source separation, which enables recycling and keeps hazardous medical items out of household waste. Waste classification studies often report near-perfect accuracy on random image splits, which put photographs of one item in both training and test sets. To measure how well classifiers recognise items they have never seen, we built BanglaWaste-23, with 4,160 images of 172 objects in 23 household and medical waste classes from Bangladesh. Every image also records which physical item it shows. We evaluated 11 ImageNet-pretrained models and two models trained from scratch (a compact custom network and MobileNetV3-L) using object-disjoint five-fold cross-validation and a random split. On unseen objects, the best pretrained model reached a macro-averaged F1 score of 98.8%, and six models were statistically tied. We introduce ApexID, a hierarchical SphereFace2-based loss that integrates global identity discrimination with balanced animal-category supervision. ApexID achieves a dataset-averaged Rank-1 accuracy of 64.89% with ConvNeXt-Small and an mAP of 32.54% with MIEW-ID, outperforming their pretrained reference models. These findings support hierarchical category-identity learning to improve transfer to unseen datasets and species.

UniDR: Learning To Generate Anatomical Deformations From Images via Unified Diffusion Modeling and Registration

This paper presents a novel framework, Unified Diffusion modeling and Registration (UniDR), which learns to generate arbitrary anatomical deformations from images with preserved topology. Existing approaches often model the generative process separately from registration-based deformation estimation; hence making them prone to propagated errors from the registration stage. To address this limitation, we propose a unified paradigm based on a conditional diffusion model over deformation fields that are jointly optimized from the registration process. A principled registration-guided score-matching objective function is then introduced to align the diffusion process with the deformation distribution induced by registration. As a result, our UniDR establishes an explicit connection between generation and registration: the diffusion model enables exploration of the space of plausible anatomical deformations, whereas registration provides structured supervision for learning transformations consistent with observed images. Once trained, UniDR is able to sample diverse, anatomically plausible transformations conditioned on any single input image. We evaluate UniDR across multiple real-world applications and demonstrate its effectiveness in generating high-fidelity image samples compared to the state-of-the-art. Additionally, we demonstrate that these generated samples improve performance in downstream medical image analysis and vision tasks.

Ref2Light: From One Image to Interactive Glasses-Free 3D Characters

Animating character illustrations on light-field displays demands coherent binocular parallax, real-time pose articulation, and fine-grained expression control. However, existing 2D animation and 3D reconstruction pipelines cannot combine these with rapid character switching. We present Ref2Light, a feed-forward framework that maps a single A-pose illustration and continuous pose and expression controls to display-native light-field animation, without per-character 3D reconstruction, rigging, or test-time optimization. Our key insight is to decompose light-field synthesis into static identity, view-dependent parallax, and control-driven articulation. A shared encoder caches multi-scale identity features once per character for rapid switching. A conditional decoder then synthesizes ordered angular anchors from these features via backward texture transport and direct color synthesis. A global-local control scheme deforms the anchors with head-body controls and uses the updated correspondence fields to track facial regions across views for masked local facial refinement. Finally, calibrated differentiable subpixel interlacing converts the anchors into the native panel raster, enabling end-to-end native supervision without rendering all views at full resolution. On held-out VRoid identities, Ref2Light reduces head-body LPIPS by 20.1%-55.6% over 2D and 3D baselines and lowers non-target change MAE in mouth closure by 95.6%-96.5% versus monolithic decoding, while running at 34.0-40.1 FPS for 1440x2560 native rasters on a consumer GPU. DeclareViT: Decoupling Early Classification from Feature Refinement with Exit Query for Efficient ViTs

Vision Transformers achieve remarkable recognition performance but incur substantial computational cost during inference, limiting their use in latency- and compute-constrained deployment scenarios. Early-exit inference offers a promising solution by allowing easy samples to terminate at intermediate layers. However, existing approaches typically couple early classification with the shared backbone, requiring intermediate representations to support both early classification and subsequent feature refinement. Under joint training, the corresponding optimization objectives jointly update shared backbone parameters, which can lead to conflicting gradient directions. Motivated by this observation, we introduce DeclareViT, a framework that decouples early classification from feature refinement. Each exit uses a dedicated query to read intermediate features through one-way cross-attention without modifying the backbone token stream. The query branches are optimized for early classification while the backbone and final classifier remain frozen. Across ViT, DeiT, and Swin on CIFAR-100, ImageNet-1K, and Food-101, DeclareViT consistently establishes a state-of-the-art accuracy-efficiency trade-off: It achieves 1.58 times to 2.57 times multiply-accumulate (MAC)-based speed-up with only a 0.39-1.91 percentage-point accuracy drop. Wall-clock measurements on CPU and GPU further demonstrate practical inference acceleration. Code is available at https://github.com/TUDa-HWAI/DeclareViT.

PROPS: Can Visual Generators Keep the Current Look of a Prop?

Many generation tasks draw the next image from a visual history, such as storyboards, illustrated stories and multi-turn editing. A new request often states what happens next without restating how earlier objects look. Instead of recovering an object's current look from the history, generators often redraw it from the request alone or bring back an outdated look, and the error can carry into later steps. We call this failure prop drift. Existing evaluations rarely isolate it, because similarity to the history rewards copying and a judge that sees a single history cannot tell a look recovered from that history from one the generator happens to prefer. We introduce PROPS, a benchmark of 2,257 pairs of twin histories that end on the same request but show one prop in two different looks. A pair passes only if the generator draws the matching look on each side and carries out the request, so a look preferred by the generator or the judge passes at most one side. All 34 open and closed systems we test show prop drift, and even systems that carry out the requested action on most sides often lose the current look of the prop. Swapping the planners and editors of agents further shows that both history understanding and image generation limit agent performance. We also build PROPS-Train, 80,250 training chains in four tracks whose targets recover an earlier state or carry scripted changes forward, and fine-tuning on it clearly improves all four tested models. Both datasets will be publicly released.

How Reliable is AI-Generated Audio Description? A Multilingual Expert Evaluation

Audio description (AD) makes visual media, such as video, accessible to blind or low-vision (BLV) audiences, but the high cost of professional production limits coverage. Due to recent advances in AI, numerous approaches to automatically generate intermediate AD scripts and/or final AD audios have been proposed. We present an expert evaluation of AI-generated AD scripts for movies in Italian and German that compares two AI systems, a GPT-4o- and a Qwen-based pipeline, against human-authored descriptions. Professional audio describers rated description quality and annotated errors using a detailed error taxonomy that includes content, character, fluency and coherence errors. The results show that while GPT-4o outperforms the Qwen models, both AI systems still fall short of human professional quality: the generated descriptions show substantially higher error rates and lower quality ratings. Based on the error annotations, we identify two key areas that require improvement: 1) character references, which includes both issues with identification and coherent naming, and 2) content errors and hallucinations, where the AD misrepresents or invents events. These error types are particularly critical, as they directly affect the viewer's ability to follow the narrative.
